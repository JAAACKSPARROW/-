Abstract

The application of peptides as inhibitors of skin aging is a promising area of research. Previous researches have predominantly focused on extracting anti-aging peptides from the collagen of specific animals, while large-scale rapid screening and analysis of the structure–activity relationships of these peptides have been scarcely reported. In the present investigation, we developed a machine learning model for screening potential anti-skin-aging peptides (PASAPs), achieving a Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.927 ± 0.044 and balanced accuracy (BACC) of 0.963 ± 0.022. These metrics surpassed those of the existing PeptideRanker model, which is widely used in bioactive peptide studies. Based on in silico screening, we identified and synthesized six novel PASAPs derived from tilapia collagen: KKHVWFGE, NGTPGAMGPR, PGAAGLKGDR, DGAPGPKGDR, TGPVGMPGAR, and GAPGGAGGVGEPGR. In vitro assays revealed that all six peptides exhibited significant inhibitory activity against aging-related enzymes, with the most pronounced effects on elastase and collagenase. A comprehensive analysis of the C-terminal amino acid residues indicated that the presence of arginine (R) at the C-terminus notably enhanced peptide binding to aging-related enzymes. This enhancement was attributed to an increased number of hydrogen bonds and stronger chemical interactions, which augmented the aging-related enzyme inhibitory activity of the peptides. In summary, this study proposed an effective strategy for discovering PASAPs from collagen and validated the machine learning model through experimental evidence. Structure–activity relationship insights can guide the synthesis of bioactive peptides and the selection of proteases for bioactive peptide production.

1 Introduction

Skin aging is a biological process caused by intrinsic (natural cellular senescence) and extrinsic factors (environmental pollution and photoaging) [1, 2]. Specifically, it involves altered activity of skin-derived cells in biosynthesizing collagen and hyaluronic acid, as well as the concomitant production of several aging-related enzymes (e.g., collagenase (MMP-1), elastase, tyrosinase, and hyaluronidase) that contribute to the degradation of skin proteins [3]. Blemishes, rough texture, wrinkles, mottled pigmentation, and significant loss of skin elasticity are the most common changes associated with the deterioration of skin appearance [4]. In addition, signaling pathways in cells are closely related to skin aging. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays a critical role in regulating various cellular processes, including cell growth, proliferation, and anti-apoptosis. Abnormalities in this pathway have been associated with skin aging. During the process of skin aging, ultraviolet (UV)-induced oxidative stress activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, which in turn promotes the activation of downstream target genes. This process serves to inhibit apoptosis and maintain skin cell homeostasis. However, overactivation of the PI3K/AKT pathway may result in increased release of inflammatory factors, thereby exacerbating skin inflammation and photoaging [5]. The Nrf2 signaling pathway serves as a central regulatory mechanism against oxidative stress and is imperative for protection against skin aging. Nrf2 scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigates oxidative damage by modulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes and detoxification enzymes. In the process of cutaneous aging, the activation of Nrf2 has been shown to inhibit UV-induced photoaging, reduce melanin production, and repair skin barrier function [6]. In this context, the cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and food industries have shown interest in bioactive compounds with skin-protective and therapeutic properties [1, 7, 8]. In addition, there is growing consumer demand for naturally sourced products because of their lower biotoxicity compared to chemically synthesized products [9].

Collagen is widely used in the food, medicine, tissue engineering, and other fields. However, its high molecular weight limits its direct absorption and utilization. In contrast, hydrolyzed collagen peptides exhibit stronger biological activity and higher absorption rates [10, 11]. Owing to their small size, proliferative effects on skin fibroblasts, and potential anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties, collagen peptides are used as dietary supplements to treat skin aging [12].

Nevertheless, rapid identification of potential anti-skin-aging peptides (PASAPs) from collagen remains challenging. Conventionally, identification of such peptides has relied on animal experiments to assess the expression of proteins such as MMP-1 and MMP-3 [13]. However, this experimental approach is inherently time-consuming. Additionally, identification of peptide sequences following protease hydrolysis is costly [14]. In recent years, advances in bioinformatics have enabled the computational prediction of peptide bioactivity, physicochemical properties, toxicological properties, enzymatic hydrolysis properties, and bioavailability [15]. Subsequently, the selected peptides were chemically synthesized and validated for in vitro activity. The integration of computer simulation and in vitro methods has emerged as a more effective strategy for discovering new bioactive peptides than traditional methods. However, most studies employing computer-assisted screening of bioactive peptides have utilized existing machine learning models. Notably, a significant proportion of these models have not been validated through wet experiments, potentially leading to inaccurate screening or exclusion of significant bioactive peptides, thereby increasing experimental costs. For instance, the PeptideRanker tool has been used to retrieve antioxidant peptides from pearl meat [16], with a specificity of 0.784 and a Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.51 for predicting short peptides [17]. These metrics may not be sufficient for predicting bioactive peptides. Therefore, developing a classification model with high accuracy and high Matthews correlation coefficient (indicating high stability) is essential to facilitate the rapid screening and development of anti-skin-aging peptides (ASAPs).

In this work, we first developed a classification model based on confident learning and ESM-2 embedding to screen peptides with potential anti-skin-aging activity. Second, we analyzed the major collagen sequences from mammals, poultry, and marine animals using computer-simulated hydrolysis to investigate the potential of collagen as a precursor for producing PASAPs. Subsequently, these peptides were screened using machine learning modeling and molecular docking techniques to identify six anti-skin-aging peptides that were synthesized for further evaluation, and their in vitro inhibitory activities of these six PASAPs against aging-related enzymes were investigated. Additionally, we used computer technology to analyze in detail the effect of the C-terminal amino acid composition of the peptides on their anti-aging activity, which is the first of its kind in the study of the structure–activity relationship of ASAPs. This research lays the foundation for producing bioactive peptides using collagen and demonstrates the effectiveness of machine-learning models in the field of bioactive peptide research.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Materials

Aging-related enzymes including collagenase, hyaluronidase (HAase), tyrosinase, and elastase were obtained from Chentong Biochemical Technology Co. (Hangzhou, China). N-succinyl-Ala-Ala-Ala-Ala-p-nitroanilide (AAAPVN) was purchased from Bomei Biology Co. (Hefei, China). Peptides with > 95% purity, including KKHVWFGE, NGTPGAMGPR, PGAAGLKGDR, DGAPGPKGDR, TGPVGMPGAR, and GAPGGAGGVGEPGR, were synthesized by Nanjing GenScript Biotechnology Co. (Nanjing, China). A tyrosinase enzyme activity test kit was purchased from Nanjing Jianchen Haihao Biotechnology Co. (Nanjing, China).

2.2 Development of classification models for ASAPs

2.2.1 Data collection and cleaning

To ensure the high-quality dataset for training, the ASAPs dataset used in this research was sourced from an existing database [18]. This database comprises data from experimentally validated anti-aging peptides, as documented in the literature. We screened this database for ASAPs using keywords such as “skin-aging” and “aging associated skin laxity”. The final dataset used in this study comprised 210 ASAPs and 210 random peptides. The details of the dataset are available at https://github.com/RH0627/skinaging_predictor_ZJU. The entire model development process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

One particular application of confident learning is CleanLab, which can identify noisy or incorrect labels in datasets. For the first time in the field of ASAPs, CleanLab was used to generate a new high-quality dataset. CleanLab operates as a data-centric AI framework that leverages robust machine-learning capabilities to detect outliers and address inconsistencies in complex experimental data [19].

To clean the dataset, we used logistic regression (LR) as the initial machine learning (ML) model in conjunction with CleanLab to detect and remove noise labels. During the cleaning process, ESM-2 was employed to generate peptide representations for the LR model. After cleaning, 339 samples remained for the model development, comprising 161 ASAPs and 178 random peptides. The cleaned dataset was split into training and testing sets in a 4:1 ratio. To evaluate CleanLab’s capability to clean the ASAP dataset, we compared the performance of the model before and after cleaning.

2.2.2 Peptide embeddings

The most recent pre-trained protein language model (pLM) version, ESM-2, was developed using the UR50/D 2021_04 dataset. It consists of six distinct pLMs with different parameters ranging from 48 layers and 5120 output embeddings to six layers and 320 output embeddings. Considering the limitations of dataset size, runtime, and dimensionality curse of the ASAPs dataset, this study selected the pLM with 320 output embeddings. The pre-trained pLM can be accessed at https://github.com/facebookresearch/esm. The detailed results of this embedding method can be accessed at https://github.com/RH0627/skinaging_predictor_ZJU.

2.2.3 Model development and evaluation

In this investigation, we compared and evaluated five ML algorithms and one DL algorithm from the scikit-learn libraries [20] and Keras (http://www.keras.io). The five ML algorithms are logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), and support vector machine (SVM) [20, 21]. One DL algorithm used in the comparison is long-short-term memory networks (LSTM).

Owing to the differences between the ML and DL models, different approaches have been used for hyperparameter selection and performance evaluation. The ML models were tuned using tenfold cross-validation (tenfold CV) for each hyperparameter combination. The GridSearchCV function provided in the scikit-learn library was combined with tenfold CV to search for optimal hyperparameters of the models. The evaluation metric used in GridSearchCV was the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which was chosen for its ability to accurately recognize both positive and negative samples. Owing to the large number of hyperparameters and computational intensity of DL networks, using the traditional GridSearchCV function is not feasible. Therefore, the DL models adopt a manual adjustment strategy for hyperparameter tuning. Table S2 lists the respective optimal hyperparameter configurations of the six models along with an analysis of their performance. These six models were further assessed using an independent test dataset. To ensure the robustness of the model performance and to avoid any biases caused by dataset partitioning, we employed random dataset partitioning. Each model underwent ten rounds of training and evaluation on both the training and testing datasets. The model performance was evaluated using several metrics, including balanced accuracy (BACC), precision, recall, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). The entire model development process was carried out on a local PC using JupyterNotebook (python3.9). Details on the development and data can be found at https://github.com/RH0627/skinaging_predictor_ZJU.

2.2.4 App development

A user-friendly PASAPs prediction program, based on the Python Tkinter library, was developed. Users can upload single sequences or XLSX files to predict the ASAPs. The complete app can be obtained from Github (https://github.com/RH0627/skin_aging_predictor_app), where a comprehensive manual is available, detailing the installation process and utilization guidelines.

2.3 Evaluation of collagen as PASAPs precursor using computer simulation

2.3.1 Profiles of potential anti-skin-aging activity

To evaluate the capacity of precursor proteins to release PASAPs, the 'enzymatic hydrolysis' tool [22] from BIOPEP (http://www.uwm.edu.pl/biochemia/index.php/pl/biopep) was used for selected animal collagens. Both collagen α-1 (I) and collagen α-2 (I) chains from the selected animal sources were hydrolyzed, and the products were predicted using the model constructed in Sect. 2.2 to assess the potential for the release of PASAPs. The predicted total potential anti-skin-aging capacity was used as a metric to assess the potential anti-skin-aging capacity of various enzymatic hydrolysis products. Given that collagen is primarily composed of α-1 (I) and α-2 (I) chains in a 2:1 ratio, it can be postulated that peptides produced by the hydrolysis of one unit of collagen using a specific enzyme should also be derived from these proteins in the same ratio. The Predicted Total Anti-skin-aging Capacity (PTASAC) was defined as a quantitative approach to characterize the effectiveness of specific enzymes in hydrolyzing collagen and producing PASAPs:

$${\text{PTASAC}} = {2}*{\text{A1}} + {\text{A2}}$$

where A1 indicates the number of peptides in the α-1 (I) chains that are predicted to be PASAPs after proteolysis. Similarly, A2 denotes the corresponding number of PASAPs derived from the α-2 (I) chains.

2.3.2 Collagen sequence alignment by BLAST analysis

Homology analysis was conducted on collagen α-1 (I) and α-2 (I) chains from selected animal sources. The BLASTp program was used to perform a Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST, http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) analysis.

2.3.3 Analysis of tilapia collagen peptide against skin aging

Research has demonstrated that peptides with fewer than 50 amino acids exhibit enhanced biological activity in vivo [23]. Therefore, this study focused on peptides with lengths of 50 µm or less in the enzymatic hydrolysis products of tilapia collagen. The enzymatic hydrolysis of tilapia collagen was performed using the 33 enzymes described in Sect. 2.3.1, and the resulting peptides were assessed for their potential anti-skin-aging activity. The capacity of these 33 enzymes to generate PASAPs was evaluated using the ASAP Ratio (ASAPR). The ASAPR was calculated using the following formula:

$${\text{ASAPR}} = {\text{CASAP}}/{\text{total peptides}}$$

where CASAP refers to the number of polypeptides of length 50 or less produced by each enzyme cleavage that are predicted to be PASAPs, and total peptides refers to the total number of polypeptides of length 50 or less produced by each enzyme cleavage.

Subsequently, V-8 protease (pH 4), clostrain, and thrombin were selected based on their ability to mimic protein hydrolysis. These enzymes were further combined (two or three enzymes at a time) to generate PASAPs from tilapia collagen with the maximum efficiency.

2.3.4 Identification sequences and physicochemical properties of PASAPs

The PASAP sequences were retrieved from protease hydrolysis results. Their physicochemical properties, including isoelectric point, solubility, and hydrophobicity, were characterized using the Innovagen tool (http://www.innovagen.com/proteomics-tools). The toxicity prediction of peptides was performed using the ToxinPred tool [24]. The final target PASAPs should be non-toxic and water-soluble. The novelty of peptide sequence was achieved by BLASTp comparisons with existing anti-aging peptide databases and amino acid composition comparisons.

2.4 Analysis of structure–activity relationship

The investigation of structure–activity relationships can serve as a reference for the efficient selection of hydrolases or the design of new bioactive peptides. Structure–activity studies of PASAPs were conducted with reference to existing literature [25,26,27]. The methodological approaches used to study the structure–activity relationships included the following: addition or deletion of amino acid residues at both ends of the peptide, substitution of amino acid residues at key positions, and molecular docking.

2.5 Molecular docking

Molecular docking was employed to investigate the intermolecular interactions between the peptides and anti-aging enzymes, including collagenase, hyaluronidase, tyrosinase, and elastase. The 3D structures of these enzymes—collagenase (PDB ID: 2Y6I, from Clostridium histolyticum), elastase (PDB ID: 1BRU, from porcine), tyrosinase (PDB ID: 2Y9X, from Agaricus bisporus), hyaluronidase (PDB ID: 2PE4, from human), and MMP-1 (PDB ID:1HFC, from human)—were downloaded from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Human collagen I chain (PDB ID: 1Y7D, α (I) chain, GPOGPOGFOGERGPOGPOGPO, single chain), AAAPVN, dopa, and hyaluronic acid (all from the PubChem database) were used as natural substrates for comparison. The energy-minimized structures of the selected active PASAPs were prepared using the ChemDraw software. Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina [28]. A semi-flexible docking approach was employed, allowing PASAPs to remain flexible while maintaining the receptor in a rigid state [29]. The dimensions of the grid box size were equally set to 24 Å for collagenase, 20 Å for elastase, 26 Å for tyrosinase, 50 Å for HAase and 20 Å for MMP-1. The grid center x, y, and z coordinates had the following values: (I) 24.1, − 2.7, and 15.9 (collagenase), (II) 23.2, 47.7, and 17.1 (elastase), (III) − 10.0, − 28.8, and − 43.6 (tyrosinase), (IV) − 41.9, − 21.7, and − 16.3 (HAase), and (V) 25.2, 22.4, and 24.3 (MMP-1). The exhaustiveness value was set to 16, while the remaining parameters were kept at the program’s default values. The binding energy was used to predict the affinity of the peptides for the receptors, where a larger absolute value of the binding energy indicates a greater affinity between the ligand and receptor. Following molecular docking, all potential PASAPs were assigned binding energy values. Binding energies were standardized to derive the final score for each peptide. The peptides with the top six final scores were selected for in vitro validation of their aging-related enzymes inhibitory activity. Additionally, Discovery Studio (version 2019, Dassault Systèmes, San Diego, USA) was used to visualize the 2D and 3D structures of the molecular interactions between peptides and receptors.

2.6 PASAPs synthesis

The six peptides screened by molecular docking were synthesized by Nanjing GenScript Biotechnology Co. (Nanjing, China) using solid-phase synthesis methods at > 95% purity. Peptide-sequences and purity were verified using HPLC and mass spectrometry. The synthesized peptides were subsequently used in in vitro aging-related enzymes inhibitory activity assays.

2.7 Aging-related enzymes inhibitory activities assay

2.7.1 Elastase inhibitory assay

Elastase inhibitory activity of six synthesized peptides was assessed in vitro using a porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE) inhibitory assay [30]. In brief, PPE was dissolved in sterile water to make a 100 U/mL solution. Moreover, AAAPVN was dissolved in 0.2 M Tris–HCl buffer at pH 8.0. The peptides were incubated with PPE for 25 min before being treated with AAAPVN to start the reaction. After another 10 min of incubation, the absorbance at 410 nm was measured.

2.7.2 Tyrosinase inhibitory assay

The activity of mushroom tyrosinase was measured using a commercial test kit. The enzyme was added at a concentration of 1250 U/mL to determine the inhibition rate.

2.7.3 Collagenase inhibitory assay

The collagenase inhibitory experiment was carried out as described by Wang et al. [31]. In brief, the bacterium's collagenase was first dissolved in the buffer. Collagenase solution (0.8 U/L), peptide solution, and tricine buffer solution were combined for an initial 30 min incubation at 25 °C. After the initial incubation, 1 mM N-(3-[2-Furyl]acryloyl)-Leu-Gly-Pro-Ala (FALGAP), a collagenase substrate, was added to all solutions and incubated for another 15 min at 25 °C. A UV spectrophotometer was used to measure the absorbance values of the sample and control solutions at 340 nm in comparison to a blank solution.

2.7.4 Hyaluronidase inhibitory assay

The activity of the hyaluronidase enzyme was determined using the method described by Nantarat et al. [32]. Briefly, the enzyme solution was rapidly added to a pre-prepared mixture of sodium formate (200 mM) and hyaluronic acid solution (12.3 mM) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 min. The change in absorbance was measured at 232 nm to calculate the enzymatic conversion rate of hyaluronic acid to N-acetylglucosamine.

2.8 Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). One-way ANOVA was performed on the data using SPSS Statistics (version 24, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with the significance level set at P < 0.05.

3 Results and discussion

3.1 Development and evaluation of classification models for PASAPs

3.1.1 Dataset cleaning and peptide embedding analysis

High-quality datasets are essential for model development and impact the generalization performance of the model. During the data cleaning process with CleanLab, the dataset was divided into two groups based on the labels of the benchmark dataset. These groups were then fitted using the LR model. The cleaning results for noisy data involved outputting the predicted probability for each peptide sequence, along with their corresponding labels. CleanLab assigned “True” labels to reliable samples while identifying and removing noisy data points. Finally, 339 high-quality data points were retained. Compared with our initial model construction, the cleaning procedure significantly enhanced the performance of the model.

CleanLab has been demonstrated to be highly reliable for imbalanced datasets [14]. In our preliminary assessments, we benchmarked CleanLab against alternative modeling techniques, such as SVM and LGBM. It was discovered that the LR model exhibited the most suitable fit throughout the modeling phase and was therefore selected.

To evaluate the effectiveness of CleanLab in predicting ASAPs within peptide embeddings, we compared model performance before and after cleaning. Figure 2 shows that after removing noisy data using CleanLab, the model exhibited significant improvement in all four evaluation metrics (P < 0.05). For example, when assessing MCC, an indicator of the overall quality of the model, the performance of the logistic LR model improved by 177.70% after cleaning (from 0.269 ± 0.169 to 0.822 ± 0.172). This further confirms the effectiveness of CleanLab for cleaning ASAPs datasets [14].

Feature engineering is a difficult part of the model development. Model performance depends on the ability to extract sufficient and useful features from basic peptide sequences. An important component of this research was the use of the advanced protein language model (pLM) ESM-2. The foundation of ESM-2 is bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)-style encoders, which are well-known for producing different word representations based on context. The ESM-2 model uses hundreds of millions of protein sequences to optimize the final hidden layer output features. Consequently, for peptides of any length, ESM-2 produced the same dimensional embedding. For example, for a peptide with n residues, ESM-2 produces vectors with dimensions n × 1 × 320 [33, 34]. This approach avoids the disadvantages of traditional peptide embedding methods, which require equal peptide lengths [34]. Moreover, conventional peptide embedding methods, such as amino acid composition (AAC) and dipeptide composition (DPC), which are based on amino acid combinations, often suffer from peptide sequence loss, inadequate peptide description, or dimensional disaster, resulting in sparse tensors for short peptide sequences. Additionally, these combination-based peptide embeddings fail to account for the physical and chemical properties of peptides, resulting in insufficient peptide descriptions [35]. Recent studies have also shown that ESM-2 has significant advantages over traditional peptide-embedding methods [14, 36].

3.1.2 Model performance comparison

Five ML methods and one popular DL method were used to develop models for predicting peptide potential anti-skin-aging properties. The cross-validation results for the six models are listed in Table S2. The performance of the models was evaluated using the test dataset. Additionally, the evaluation process was repeated ten times with ten different random splits of the test dataset. The details of the results are shown in Fig. 2 and Table S2.

We conducted extensive hyperparameter optimization to determine the optimal model architectures and their appropriate hyperparameters for the ESM-2 embedding strategy. The optimal combinations were as follows: ESMclean + SVM (BACC = 0.963 ± 0.022, recall = 0.976 ± 0.024, precision = 0.951 ± 0.030, and MCC = 0.927 ± 0.044). ESMclean + RNN achieved a model performance similar to that of the SVM. However, given that the training dataset comprised only 339 samples, there was a concern about overfitting with the DL model. Consequently, SVM was selected, which can represent nonlinear relationships and a good performance on small-scale datasets.

Currently, there are few report develops classification models for predicting PASAPs. Therefore, no state-of-the-art (SOTA) model can be directly compared with our model in terms of performance. However, the performance improvements reported in previous studies can be used for limited comparisons. In previous studies, PeptideRanker has been widely used to screen potential bioactive peptides, such as antioxidant peptides, from pearl meat [16, 17]. However, the MCC value predicted by the PeptideRanker model for short peptides was only 0.51. The MCC value of our ESMclean + SVM model increased by 81.76% compared with that of PeptideRanker. These results indicate that the model performs significantly better than previous models in predicting anti-skin aging properties. The validity of the model is further substantiated by in vitro experiments in Sect. 3.4, which will provide additional evidence of its effectiveness. This renders developed models appealing to researchers in the domain of bioactive peptides.

3.2 Analysis of the ability of collagen to produce PASAPs

3.2.1 Profiles of potential anti-skin-aging activity of collagen and BLAST analysis

To date, 28 different types of collagen have been genetically identified. Type I collagen is the most abundant protein in all vertebrates, found in skin, bone, and almost all connective tissues. It consists of three polypeptide chains: two α-1(I) chains and one α-2(I) chain [37]. The UniProt and NCBI databases were used to obtain α-1(I) and α-2(I) chain sequences from various animal sources for in silico hydrolysis.

The simulated hydrolysis of collagen α-1(I) and α-2(I) chains from selected animals was performed using 33 enzymes, as listed in Table S1. The hydrolysis products were predicted to have potential anti-skin-aging activity using the developed model. The ability of collagen to produce PASAPs in selected animals was evaluated using the PTASAC score. The results showed that the average PTASAC scores of bovine, porcine, chicken, salmon, and tilapia collagen were 119.55, 118.79, 120.15, 118.18, and 118.64, respectively. These results indicate that the ability of collagen to produce PASAPs does not differ significantly among animal species. Subsequent analysis using BLAST is imperative to ascertain sequence similarity among these species.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the collagen sequences from various animal sources share a high percentage of identity, with over 76% for the α-1(I) chain and over 69% for the α-2(I) chain. These observations align closely with the results reported by Hou et al. [37]. According to the BLAST analysis results, the collagen sequence similarity between mammals and poultry in the α-1(I) chain exceeded 90%, while the collagen similarity between marine organisms and mammals and poultry remained comparatively low, ranging from 76 to 80%. Conversely, collagens from the two fish species exhibited 84% similarity, a significant departure from the similarity observed between the other three species of disparate animals. The same trend was observed in the α-2(I) chain. Given the comparable viability of producing PASAPs from diverse animal sources and the diminished collagen similarity observed in marine organisms compared to mammals and poultry, it is postulated that marine animals possess a higher propensity to generate PASAPs characterized by substantial sequence divergences [38]. Consequently, tilapia, a species that has received comparatively less attention in previous studies, was selected as the animal source for subsequent simulated enzymatic hydrolysis.

3.2.2 Single and complex enzyme proteolysis and screening of PASAPs in silico

Enzymatic hydrolysis using proteases represents the most efficient method for releasing bioactive peptides from proteins [39, 40]. To identify the most suitable proteases for simulated production of PASAPs from tilapia collagen, extensive enzymatic hydrolysis of tilapia collagen was performed using computer-aided simulations.

Initially, computer-simulated enzymatic hydrolysis of the α-1(I) and α-2(I) chains of tilapia was conducted using 33 enzymes. The efficiency of the active peptides generated by each enzyme was analyzed to identify the most efficient way to release PASAPs. The methodology for evaluating the efficiency is outlined in Sect. 2.3.3. As shown in Fig. 4, from the hydrolysis results of single enzyme, three enzymes—V-8 protease (pH = 4), clostripain, and thrombin—catalyzed hydrolysis to produce PASAPs with the highest efficiency, which were as high as 75.9%, 74.3%, and 71.9%, respectively. Therefore, the release of PASAPs hydrolyzed by complex enzymes was further investigated using these three proteases.

The combination of protease hydrolysis may facilitate the release of desired peptides in a more efficacious manner [40]. Consequently, these three proteases were selected for further investigation to ascertain their combined effects on simulated protein hydrolysis. Three two-enzyme combinations and one three-enzyme combination were utilized to simulate the hydrolysis of tilapia collagen in anticipation of the most efficient release of PASAPs. As shown in Table 1, neither the dual-enzyme nor the triple-enzyme combination reached a similar efficiency of releasing PASAPs in comparison to the single enzyme. However, they augmented the number of released PASAPs [41]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the ability of the enzyme to generate a greater number of short peptides, although only a limited proportion of these peptides possess potential anti-skin-aging activity. The V-8 protease (pH = 4) yielded the most efficient release of PASAPs. Therefore, the peptide sequences produced by the V-8 protease (pH = 4) through stimulated hydrolysis of tilapia collagen were selected for further investigation.

3.3 Molecular docking of selected peptides with related enzymes

Sixty peptides derived from the hydrolysis of tilapia collagen using V-8 protease (pH = 4) were selected for analysis. After removing repetitive sequences, 26 peptides with hydrophobic features, high water solubility, and non-toxicity were selected using the tools described in Sect. 2.3.4. High water solubility, hydrophobicity, and nontoxicity are key factors in the digestion and metabolism of peptides in vivo [42]. Moreover, we proceeded to analyze the screened sequences against the sequences in the anti-aging peptide database using BLASTp. This analysis did not reveal any significantly related sequences. Furthermore, an exhaustive search was conducted in existing bioactive peptide databases (BIOPEP-UWM), which yielded no duplicates, thereby confirming the novelty of the sequences under scrutiny. In regard to the variability of the sequences, amino acid composition was utilized for analysis. The results demonstrated that the PASAPs under investigation exhibited a higher proportion of G, A, and E and a lower proportion of C, F, L, S, and W when compared to the anti-aging peptide databases. Peptides containing I and Q were not identified in the screened peptides. This phenomenon may be attributed to the incorporation of tilapia as a collagen raw material, which predominantly contains G (Table S3). Consequently, the PASAPs obtained from our screening exhibited substantial disparities from existing anti-aging peptides with regard to amino acid species and content. Consequently, these 26 peptides were selected for further molecular docking simulations to evaluate their potential anti-skin-aging properties.

Molecular docking has been widely utilized to predict and analyze bioactive peptides with the capacity to interact with enzymes associated with potential anti-skin-aging properties at the molecular level [31, 43]. The extant literature indicates that the degree of tightness of intermolecular combinations is correlated with the binding energies. Lower binding energies are indicative of tighter ligand-receptor binding and more stable complex formation [16]. Twenty six different PASAPs were subjected to molecular docking with collagenase, hyaluronidase, tyrosinase, and elastase using a computer to facilitate further screening and identify the PASAPs for subsequent in vitro antioxidant activity validation. Table 2 depicts binding energies of the 26 peptides with respect to the relevant receptors. Peptides were ranked according to their binding energies, as outlined in Sect. 2.5. The six peptide sequences with the lowest binding energies were KKHVWFGE, NGTPGAMGPR, PGAAGLKGDR, DGAPGPKGDR, TGPVGMPGAR, and GAPGGAGGVGEPGR. The highest-ranked peptide, KKHVWFGE, was selected for further analysis to elucidate the mechanism of action of PASAPs derived from tilapia collagen.

As illustrated in Fig. 5a, amino acid residues LEU550, HIS527, HIS523, ASP491, and TYR607 of collagenase form five hydrogen bonds with KKHVWFGE. Additionally, residues ASP554 and TRP604 engaged in attractive charge interactions with KKHVWFGE. These residues, particularly HIS523, ASP491, and TYR607, match those identified in previous studies as key binding sites for collagenase [43] and play a crucial role in regulating collagenase activity. As shown in Fig. 5b, elastase residues SER190, SER214, GLY216, SER217, TYR35, and ARG63 establish eight hydrogen bonds with KKHVWFGE, whereas ARG39, ARG143, and SER195 form attractive charge interactions. Notably, SER190, SER214, GLY216, and SER217 align with elastase-binding sites reported in the literature, highlighting their significance in elastase activity [43]. Figure 5c shows that tyrosinase residues ASN81, ALA323, VAL283, and MET280 form five hydrogen bonds with KKHVWFGE, and GLU103 forms attractive charge interactions. These residues, including ASN81, ALA323, and VAL283, correspond to tyrosinase-binding sites, as documented previously [43]. As depicted in Fig. 5d, residues TRP141, TYR202,ARG134, THR143, and TYR85 of HAase form seven hydrogen bonds with KKHVWFGE, and residues GLU131, ASP292, LYS144, and ARG134 form attractive charges with KKHVWFGE. TYR202 and GLU131, identified in related studies [31], are integral to HAase-binding sites and influence HAase activity. As shown in Fig. 5e, residues HIS228, ALA184, ASN180, PRO173 and PRO238 of MMP-1 form five hydrogen bonds with KKHVWFGE, and residues HIS222, GLU219, and ASP175 form attractive charges with KKHVWFGE. HIS228, GLU219, and PRO238, identified in related studies [44], are integral to MMP-1-binding sites and influence MMP-1 activity. Interestingly, according to the molecular docking results, the zinc ion (ZN275) in MMP-1 also binds to the carbonyl oxygen of the peptide to form a hydrogen bond, which reinforces that our screened peptide can occupy the active site of MMP-1 and thus inhibit the enzyme activity.

To further analyze whether our peptides can compete with natural substrates for inhibition in a physiological environment, we analyzed the binding energies between aging-related enzymes and their natural substrates. In the case of collagenase and MMP-1, human collagen I chain was utilized as the natural substrate for molecular docking, yielding binding energies of − 6.4 kcal/mol and − 4.8 kcal/mol, respectively. For elastase, given the high molecular weight of elastin, the common substrate of the enzyme, AAAPVN, was selected as a control, yielding a binding energy of − 4.9 kcal/mol. For tyrosinase, the substrate was determined to be dopa, exhibiting a binding energy of − 6.1 kcal/mol. For hyaluronidase, hyaluronic acid was utilized as the natural substrate, exhibiting a binding energy of − 7.1 kcal/mol. The molecular docking results of the peptides revealed that the majority of peptides exhibited lower binding energies (i.e., stronger binding) compared to natural products, particularly MMP-1, elastase, and tyrosinase.

Overall, the results suggest that the peptides examined, particularly those with experimental validation, possess the capacity to inhibit the activity of aging-related enzymes.

3.4 In vitro aging-related enzymes inhibitory activity of synthesized peptides

The aging-related enzymes inhibitory activities of the six selected and synthesized peptides were evaluated using inhibition assays for collagenase, tyrosinase, elastase, and HAase. As shown in Fig. 6, each peptide demonstrated significant inhibitory activity against enzymes associated with skin aging. The synthesized peptides potentially released from tilapia collagen inhibited tyrosinase by 32.60 ± 3.41% to 68.63 ± 5.80%, HAase by 46.53 ± 1.66% to 80.53 ± 4.08%, elastase by 80.49 ± 3.13% to 94.39 ± 3.20%, and collagenase by 68.97 ± 1.82% to 80.12 ± 1.47%. These inhibition rates were significantly higher than those of the blank control group and achieved higher or comparable enzyme inhibitory activities (P < 0.05) to those of the positive control group (GSH). These results indicated that the peptides screened by the machine-learning model effectively inhibited aging-related enzymes. The decline in enzyme activity in the peptide-treated group can be attributed to the ability of the peptides to form strong attractive charges and hydrogen bonding interactions with these enzymes, thereby occupying the enzyme's active site and preventing substrate binding. This results in the inhibition of enzymatic reactions associated with skin aging and, consequently, skin protection from aging [45,46,47]. Additionally, collagen-derived peptides have been demonstrated to impede the expression of the MMP-1/3 pathway, thereby curtailing collagen degradation in the skin and offering protection [48].

3.5 Structure–activity relationship studies on potential anti-skin-aging properties of the collagen

After model evaluation and in vitro experiments, the validity of the developed model in predicting PASAPs was confirmed, making the structure–activity relationship analysis of the predicted peptides with potential anti-skin-aging activity more convincing. Upon examining the results of the enzymatic cleavage of tilapia collagen in Sect. 3.2, it was observed that the recognition and cleavage sites of the enzymes were most efficient in digesting PASAPs containing lysine (K), proline (E), and arginine (R), and these residues were consistently located at the C-terminus. Therefore, the structure–activity relationships in this study focused on the amino acid composition of the C-terminus of the peptides.

According to the three methods of analyzing structure–activity relationships in the existing literature (using computer simulations) [26, 27], the initial step was to select three polypeptides with K, E, and R at the C-terminus. Substitution of C-terminal amino acid residues was performed using cysteine (C). The rationale for selecting C was that polypeptides with a C-terminal cysteine constitute the smallest proportion in the anti-aging database. The aging-related enzymes inhibitory activity of the peptides was examined to determine whether the substitution weakened the activity. These peptides were sourced from the database described in Sect. 2.2 and the hydrolysis results in Sect. 2.3, all of which have been shown to have aging-related enzymes inhibitory activity. As illustrated in Table 3, molecular docking of these peptides revealed that peptides with C-terminal E and R substitutions exhibited diminished binding capacity to aging-related enzymes, suggesting that peptides with C-terminal E and R may significantly influence the binding ability of the peptides to aging-related enzymes.

Subsequently, another proven ASAP, NFF [49] was selected as a backbone, and K, E, and R were appended to its C-terminus to monitor alterations in its aging-related enzymes inhibitory activity. As shown in Table 3, the scores represent the normalized additive sum of the absolute values of each binding energy, providing a more intuitive depiction of the disparities between the values. NFFC was used to exclude the increase in aging-related enzymes inhibition activity due to an increase in peptide length. These results indicated that the potential anti-skin-aging activity of the peptide was enhanced when E and R were appended to the C-terminus of the peptide.

Moreover, we randomly selected a peptide LLL from the BIOPEP-UWM database that does not have potential anti-skin-aging activity after model prediction and then added K, E, and R to its C-terminus to determine whether these modifications could confer potential anti-skin-aging activity. As shown in Table 3, the addition of K and R to the C-terminus of LLL significantly enhanced its ability to inhibit aging-related enzymes.

Finally, the specific role of R at the C-terminus was verified. The peptide DGAPGPKGDR was selected for experimental validation due to its predicted C-terminal R and its potential anti-skin aging activity. Substitutions of R with the amino acids K and H, which possess analogous physicochemical properties to R (positively charged), were made to assess the impact on the peptide's activity. As demonstrated in Table 3, the binding capacity of the C-terminal H and K substituted peptides to aging-related enzymes was diminished, indicating that C-terminal H and K substitutions may substantially influence the binding capability of the peptides to aging-related enzymes.

In summary, the simulation results indicate that binding ability of peptides to aging-related enzymes is enhanced when the C-terminus contains an R residue. Therefore, it was postulated that the R residue is one of the key factors in determining whether a peptide has potential anti-skin-aging activity or not. To validate this hypothesis, we investigated the binding sites of peptides with C-terminal R residues and aging-related enzymes using molecular docking. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the side chain of the C-terminal R of the peptide contains –NH2, which exhibits strong electronegativity and contains a lone electron pair [50]. This functionality enables the peptide to act as a hydrogen bond donor, thereby enhancing its capacity to form hydrogen bonds with the enzyme, resulting in an increased binding ability to the relevant enzymes and, consequently, enzyme inhibition. Meanwhile, the C-terminus of the peptide has an ornithine residue at the end of the R side chain, which is positively charged at physiological pH and can form salt bridges or attractive charges with negatively charged amino acids that are stronger than hydrogen bonds. Moreover, negatively charged Glu is a key active site for a variety of aging-related enzymes [51,52,53], so peptides with an R at the C-terminus can form stronger binding states with these enzymes. For the R residue itself, L-arginine can activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, increase the expression of Nrf2 protein, inhibit the high expression of Keapl protein, and exert anti-aging effects via multiple mechanisms [6]. Additionally, –NH2 in the R side chains at both ends of the polypeptide can provide hydrogen to scavenge free radicals and realize anti-aging in an antioxidant manner [54].

As demonstrated by the aforementioned arguments, it can be inferred that proteases that utilize the C-terminal R residue as an enzyme cleavage site are potential candidates for the production of peptides with the ability to inhibit aging-related enzymes.

3.6 A brief analysis of the effect of proline hydroxylation on the binding ability of peptides to aging-related enzymes

Hydroxyproline is an essential component of collagen, while in FASTA sequences, it is represented as P, similar to proline. However, extant literature indicates that proline hydroxylation significantly affects the binding of type II collagen to hyaluronidase [55]. Therefore, we hydroxylated the P residues in the screened peptides to observe changes in their binding capacity with aging-related enzymes, providing a reference for subsequent studies.

First, we analyzed the molecular docking results of proline and hydroxyproline with aging-related enzymes. As shown in Table 4, it was indicated that hydroxyproline exhibits higher binding affinity (lower binding energy) with aging-related enzymes. This phenomenon can be attributed to the hydroxyl group in the hydroxyproline residue being more inclined to form additional hydrogen bonds with the relevant enzymes (Fig. S1). These hydroxyl groups possess the capacity to function as both hydrogen bond acceptors and hydrogen bond donors. It suggests that proline hydroxylation may enhance the binding affinity between proline and aging-related enzymes.

Furthermore, the proline residues in the five selected peptides were subjected to hydroxylation treatment. Given the minimal impact of the hydroxylation site, all proline hydroxylation strategies employed in this work utilized 4-hydroxylation [55]. It was demonstrated that hydroxylated peptides exhibited enhanced binding capacity with aging-related enzymes in Table 4, with the exception of tyrosinase. In order to explain this phenomenon at the molecular level, an analysis was conducted of the attachment sites of peptides to aging-related enzymes. The results demonstrated that following the hydroxylation of proline in the peptides, the hydroxyproline hydroxyl group, situated in close proximity to the N-terminus, formed novel hydrogen bonds with the enzyme (Fig. S2). This observation suggests that this interaction may enhance the binding capacity. However, two distinct mechanisms have been identified that facilitate the weakening of the binding energy during the attachment of the peptide to tyrosinase. The first mechanism involves the failure of either of the two hydroxyl groups of the hydroxyproline residue in the peptide to form a hydrogen bond with the tyrosinase-associated site. The second possibility is that the unhydroxylated proline at the N-terminal of the peptide can form a strong attractive charge with tyrosinase. However, when the proline is hydroxylated, this binding becomes hydrogen bonded, thus leading to a decrease in the binding capacity with tyrosinase (Figs. S2, S3).

In summary, peptides containing hydroxyproline may form more stable structures with collagenase, hyaluronidase, and elastase, thereby inhibiting the activity of these enzymes. However, analyzing the random hydroxylation positions of proline to obtain more optimized collagen peptide structures is limited in the present work. To obtain a more comprehensive and detailed study (e.g., the effect of hydroxyproline content and its position on peptide activity), it is necessary to explore the specific effects of hydroxyproline on the affinity between peptides and enzymes in follow-up works.

4 Conclusion

This research developed an integrated approach combining machine learning, computer simulation of protein hydrolysis, and molecular docking screening with in vitro evaluation to determine the potential of collagen as a source of ASAPs. Specifically, an efficient classification model for PASAPs based on ESM-2 embedding, CleanLab cleaning, and SVM was designed, which exhibited superior performance and was validated through in vitro experiments. We further demonstrated the effectiveness of screening PASAPs using a coupled machine learning and molecular docking approach, where six novel PASAPs were identified and the potential molecular binding mechanism of KKHVWFGE was explored. These peptides reduce the occurrence of enzymatic skin aging reactions and attenuate skin damage, mainly by inhibiting the activities of aging-related enzymes. Structure–activity relationship analysis revealed the importance of C-terminal R residues in enhancing the anti-skin aging activity of the peptides. This advancement not only contributes to the effective selection of hydrolytic enzymes required for peptide production but also provides a basis for the design of new bioactive peptides. In conclusion, by integrating advanced machine learning and molecular docking techniques, the developed model greatly reduces the cost and time of screening PASAPs using traditional wet experimental methods and provides a valuable reference for the study of the mechanism of action and structure–activity relationship of PASAPs.

5 Limitations

Two aspects of the study require some caution when interpreting the results. Firstly, the binding energy results of molecular docking deviate from the actual experimental results. The underlying reason for this discrepancy is that molecular docking is predicated on static conformational simulations of receptors and ligands. These simulations do not take into account dynamic conformational changes, solvent effects, or protein flexibility (e.g., induced fit effects) in organisms. For instance, certain peptides may exhibit high binding energies in docking, yet their binding is unstable due to conformational changes in the actual enzyme active site [56]. Secondly, the experimental conditions may have an effect on the enzyme inhibition activity. It is important to note that the conditions employed in in vitro enzyme inhibition experiments, such as pH, ionic strength, and temperature, may not always align with the solvent conditions of molecular docking simulations. Furthermore, the presence of other substrates or inhibitors within the enzyme reaction system may interfere with peptide binding to the enzyme [57]. Finally, peptides may exert their activity through synergistic effects on multiple targets (e.g., simultaneous inhibition of collagenase and elastase), whereas molecular docking is only directed at a single target, which may also be a consequence of the fact that docking binding energies do not exactly correspond to enzyme inhibition [58].

Secondly, the signature amino acid in collagen, hydroxyproline (Hyp), has been identified as a limitation in machine learning studies of peptides. Given its classification as a subamino acid, hydroxyproline is not included in the FASTA sequences most commonly employed for machine learning. In the dataset utilized in this work, peptides are represented as FASTA sequences, thereby precluding the consideration of hydroxyproline. However, the potential of hydroxyproline to play an inhibitory role in the process of skin aging is noteworthy. Ohara. H [59] demonstrated that hydroxyproline possesses substantial antioxidant properties. Furthermore, it was determined that the presence of hydroxyproline in collagen peptides facilitates the formation of stable peptide bonds with other amino acids. It was ascertained that these dipeptides, composed of hydroxyproline and another amino acid, exhibited efficacy in resisting protease-induced degradation. The mechanism of this resistance involves a specialized transport system that facilitates the stabilization of the dipeptides. Upon entering the bloodstream, the dipeptide fragments act as a “cellular signal” that promotes the proliferation of fibroblasts. Fibroblasts are responsible for producing collagen, hyaluronic acid, and other substances in the human body. However, the current study does not include research on machine learning of hydroxyproline. Therefore, this would be a potential direction for subsequent research.

Moreover, owing to constraints in the availability of experimental materials, the enzymes utilized in the wet experiments, with the exception of hyaluronidase, were derived from non-human species. In order to ensure consistency between the computer simulations and the wet experiments, the same enzymes were utilized in both sets of experiments. In future work, it is advised that researchers prioritize the utilization of human-derived enzymes in experiments and screening procedures, which will be more meaningful for the application of experimental results to humans.
